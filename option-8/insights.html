Capital · Succession

The founder's dilemma: business succession and ownership succession

The future of a business and the future of its ownership are connected questions. They are not always the same decision.

Founders often begin succession conversations with one question: who will run the business next? A second question deserves equal attention: how should ownership change, and on what terms?

Separate the questions

Operational leadership, shareholder interests and personal plans may call for different timelines. Treating them as one decision can narrow the options before the wider picture is understood.

Make room for a process

A considered process can clarify priorities, identify information gaps and help the relevant advisers work from a shared brief. The right path depends on the business, its owners and the people involved.

A longer view begins by naming the decisions separately, then considering how they fit together.

Private · Wealth

Selling a business is not the end of the wealth journey

A transaction can change the shape of a family's financial life. It can also create new questions about purpose, coordination and time.

After a sale, attention often shifts from building one business to stewarding a wider set of resources. That transition can touch investments, property, family priorities and professional relationships.

Bring the pieces into view

A clear picture of assets, obligations, objectives and existing advice can provide a useful starting point. From there, families can decide which questions need attention first.

Let priorities guide the pace

There is no single timetable for these decisions. Making space to understand what matters can help distinguish urgent actions from choices that benefit from further thought.

Wealth planning is an ongoing conversation, shaped by circumstances and priorities.

Legacy · Succession

When there is wealth but no natural successor

Succession can be planned around the life and wishes of the individual, even when a family successor is not part of the picture.

Some people do not have children or relatives who wish to take on responsibility. Others want to include friends, charitable causes or a wider community in their plans.

Make intentions understandable

Start by identifying what matters, who should be involved and which professional advice is needed. Clear records and conversations can help reduce uncertainty for the people who may later be asked to act.

Coordinate qualified advice

Legal and tax arrangements require advice from qualified professionals. A coordinated view can help keep those arrangements connected to the intentions they are meant to serve.

A plan can reflect chosen relationships and values, not only family structure.
